ABSTRACT
Industrial motors are critical prime movers in manufacturing plants, where unexpected failures can
cause production losses, maintenance costs and safety risks. Conventional preventive maintenance
relies heavily on fixed inspection intervals and operator experience, while single-signal monitoring can
fail to distinguish between electrical and mechanical abnormalities. This paper proposes a PLC-based
multi-sensor condition monitoring architecture that combines motor electrical current and vibration
measurements for early detection of abnormal operating conditions. The proposed system acquires
current signals through non-invasive current sensors and mechanical vibration through accelerometers,
performs signal conditioning and feature extraction, and transfers the resulting condition indicators to a
programmable logic controller for real-time decision making. Time-domain indicators such as RMS,
peak value, crest factor and variance are combined with frequency-domain information obtained using
Fast Fourier Transform analysis. A rule-based fusion layer is proposed to distinguish normal operation,
warning conditions and critical conditions, while a human-machine interface provides trend
visualization and alarm information. The approach is intended for industrial motor-driven equipment
such as pumps, compressors, fans and conveyors. The paper develops the system architecture,
monitoring variables, fault-feature relationships, PLC decision logic and an experimental validation
procedure. The principal contribution is a practical condition-monitoring framework that combines
electrical and mechanical evidence at the control level rather than relying on a single sensor. The
design is suitable for low-cost industrial implementation and provides a foundation for later integration
of predictive analytics and machine-learning methods.
Keywords: PLC, Condition Monitoring, Induction Motor, Motor Current Signature Analysis, Vibration
Analysis, Predictive Maintenance, Multi-Sensor Fusion.
1. INTRODUCTION
Electric motors are among the most widely used components in industrial production systems
because they provide the mechanical power required by pumps, compressors, fans, conveyors and
process machines. Their reliability directly affects plant availability. A motor failure may stop an entire
production line even when the failed component is relatively small compared with the overall process.
Consequently, the ability to identify abnormal motor behaviour before a complete breakdown is an
important part of modern maintenance engineering.
Traditional maintenance approaches generally include corrective maintenance and preventive
maintenance. Corrective maintenance waits for a failure, while preventive maintenance replaces or
services equipment according to a fixed time or operating-hour interval. Preventive maintenance
reduces some unexpected failures, it can also result in unnecessary component replacement when the
actual condition of the motor is still acceptable. Condition-based maintenance provides an alternative
by using measured operating information to determine when intervention is required. Recent literature
identifies current and vibration signals as two important sources of information for electrical-machine
condition monitoring. Current-based methods are attractive because current sensors can be installed
without direct mechanical access to the rotating machine, while vibration measurements provide direct

Motor Current Signature Analysis (MCSA) uses changes in the motor stator-current waveform and
spectrum to identify abnormal operating conditions. Research has shown that electrical and mechanical
faults can alter the current signature, making current analysis useful for non-invasive diagnosis
(Benbouzid, 2000; Niu, 2023). However, current measurements can be affected by load variation,
power-quality disturbances, variable speed operation and inverter switching. Vibration analysis
provides complementary information, particularly for bearings, imbalance, misalignment and
mechanical looseness. A multi-sensor approach can therefore reduce the limitations of relying on either
signal alone.
Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs) are already installed in many industrial plants for sequencing,
interlocking, motor control and process automation. This makes the PLC a practical platform for
implementing condition-monitoring logic without requiring a completely separate monitoring
infrastructure. The proposed study therefore combines current sensing, vibration sensing, signal
processing and PLC logic into one architecture. The concept follows the practical, application-oriented
structure of the supplied reference paper, but adapts the research problem from intelligent parking
allocation to industrial motor health monitoring. The reference paper similarly moves from a defined
problem, through related work and a system methodology, to performance evaluation and limitations.
1.1 Need for Intelligent Motor Condition Monitoring
Industrial motor faults do not always develop as sudden failures. Bearing degradation, shaft
misalignment, rotor abnormalities, winding problems and mechanical imbalance can progress
gradually. If abnormal conditions are detected early, maintenance can be scheduled during a planned
stoppage instead of during an unplanned production interruption. This is particularly important in
plants where several motor-driven machines share common production resources.
A single sensor, however, may not provide sufficient diagnostic confidence. For example, an increase
in vibration can be associated with bearing deterioration, imbalance, misalignment or looseness.
Similarly, an abnormal current component can originate from electrical faults or from a mechanical
load problem reflected through electromechanical coupling. Combining the two signals makes it
possible to compare electrical and mechanical evidence before a maintenance alarm is generated.
1.2 Key Innovations and Contributions
• A PLC-oriented architecture for continuous acquisition of motor current and vibration information.
• Combination of time-domain and frequency-domain features rather than dependence on a single
threshold.
• A multi-sensor decision layer that separates normal, warning and critical operating states.
• Integration of condition indicators with an HMI/SCADA-style display for maintenance personnel.
• A practical validation procedure that can be implemented on an industrial motor test rig without
requiring a high-cost monitoring platform.
2. RELATED WORK
Condition monitoring of electrical motors has developed from periodic manual inspection toward
online sensing and data-driven diagnostics. Early approaches relied on temperature, insulation testing,
audible noise and mechanical inspection. More recent approaches make use of electrical signatures,
vibration, flux, acoustic emission and combinations of these measurements. Reviews of electrical
motor condition monitoring identify current signature analysis, vibration analysis and intelligent
Page 2
--- PAGE 3 ---
diagnostic techniques as important areas of research (Gangsar and Tiwari, 2020), (Awadallah and
Morcos, 2003).
Benbouzid (2000) established the theoretical and practical basis of motor signature analysis for
identifying abnormal electrical and mechanical conditions from stator-current spectra. The method is
attractive because the current signal is available while the motor is running and can be measured
without installing a mechanical sensor on the shaft. Later work has continued to develop current-based
diagnostics for induction motors, including frequency-domain, time-frequency and artificial-
intelligence approaches (Niu, 2023), (Yakhni et al., 2023).
Vibration monitoring provides a different perspective. Mechanical faults produce characteristic
changes in vibration amplitude and frequency content. Bearing defects, imbalance, shaft misalignment
and looseness can therefore be investigated using accelerometers. Reviews of signal-based induction-
motor diagnosis show that both current and vibration signals contain useful information, but their
response to different fault mechanisms is not identical (Gangsar and Tiwari, 2020).
Yang and Kim (2006) demonstrated the value of combining current and vibration signals for induction-
motor fault diagnosis. Their work used feature extraction and data fusion to improve diagnostic
decisions, showing that combining information from the two measurement domains can support online
fault diagnosis. This provides an important foundation for the multi-sensor concept proposed in this
paper.
Recent research has also focused on variable-speed operation. Yakhni et al. (2023) note that transient
current signatures are more challenging to interpret when motor speed is not constant. This is relevant
to industrial plants using variable-frequency drives (VFDs), where current waveforms contain
switching components and operating-point changes. A practical PLC implementation should therefore
avoid depending on one fixed spectral peak and should combine signal features with operating-state
information.

2.2 Research Gap
The literature demonstrates the usefulness of current and vibration analysis, but there remains a
practical implementation gap between signal-processing research and the control systems already
available in industrial plants. Many advanced diagnostic systems depend on specialized data-
acquisition hardware or machine-learning platforms. For smaller industrial facilities, the cost,

--- PAGE 4 ---
maintenance and technical complexity of such systems can become barriers to deployment. The
research gap addressed here is therefore the development of a structured, PLC-centred multi-sensor
architecture that retains useful diagnostic information while remaining understandable to plant
engineers and maintenance technicians.
3. METHODOLOGY
The research adopts a multi-phase experimental and computational framework. The proposed
system is designed around an industrial induction motor connected to a representative mechanical load.
Current and vibration signals are acquired simultaneously under normal and controlled abnormal
conditions. Signal features are extracted, normalized and supplied to PLC decision logic. The resulting
condition state is displayed through an HMI or SCADA interface. The approach is divided into system
modelling, sensing, signal processing, PLC implementation and validation.
3.1 Proposed Multi-Sensor Architecture
The proposed architecture contains five functional layers: sensing, signal conditioning, feature
extraction, PLC decision making and operator visualization. A current transformer or Hall-effect
current sensor measures one or more phase currents. An industrial accelerometer measures radial
vibration on the motor bearing housing. The sensor outputs are conditioned to match the analogue
input requirements of the acquisition system. Where high-frequency vibration information is required,
a dedicated acquisition module may perform sampling and feature extraction before communicating
summary values to the PLC.
• Layer 1 – Sensing: motor current and vibration sensors.
• Layer 2 – Signal conditioning: filtering, scaling, isolation and analogue-to-digital conversion.
• Layer 3 – Feature extraction: RMS, peak, crest factor, variance, kurtosis and selected spectral
components.
• Layer 4 – PLC diagnosis: threshold comparison, sensor fusion, alarm classification and event
logging.
• Layer 5 – HMI/SCADA: live values, trends, alarms, motor status and maintenance
recommendation.
3.2 Motor Current Measurement
Current measurement is selected as the principal electrical signal because it is directly associated
with motor loading and can be obtained without opening the motor enclosure. For a sampled current
signal i(k), the root mean square current over a window of N samples is calculated as:
I_RMS = sqrt[(1/N) Σ i²(k)]
Changes in RMS current can indicate loading changes, phase imbalance or abnormal operating
conditions. However, RMS alone cannot reliably identify every motor fault. Therefore, the current
waveform is also transformed into the frequency domain using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). The
frequency spectrum is examined for changes in characteristic components associated with motor faults.
For variable-speed motors, the PLC system should record motor speed or VFD operating frequency
together with current features. This allows the diagnostic algorithm to interpret spectral changes
relative to the actual operating point rather than comparing all data against one fixed frequency.
3.3 Vibration Measurement
An accelerometer is mounted on the motor housing near the bearing location. The vibration signal
x(k) is sampled over a defined time window. Important time-domain features include RMS value, peak
amplitude, crest factor and kurtosis. RMS represents the overall vibration energy in the measurement

window, while crest factor relates the peak level to the RMS level. Kurtosis is useful for identifying
impulsive changes that may occur during developing bearing defects.
Crest Factor = Peak / RMS
Frequency-domain analysis is then used to identify dominant components and changes in the
vibration spectrum. The mounting position, sampling frequency and sensor bandwidth must remain
consistent during testing because changes in measurement configuration can affect the observed
values.

3.5 Signal Processing and Feature Extraction
Raw signals are first checked for sensor saturation and missing samples. A low-pass or band-pass
filter can be used to reduce unwanted noise depending on the sensor and sampling rate. The processing
window is selected so that the PLC receives sufficiently stable features without introducing excessive
detection delay. For each window, the system calculates a feature vector:
F = [I_RMS, I_peak, I_imbalance, V_RMS, V_peak, V_crest, V_kurtosis, spectral indicators]
The feature vector is normalized using baseline measurements collected while the motor is
operating under known healthy conditions. This reduces the effect of differences between motors.
Baseline values should be established separately for each critical motor because motor size, mounting
arrangement, load and operating speed influence measured values.
3.6 PLC-Based Decision Logic
The PLC implements a three-level condition classification. The normal state is assigned when the
monitored features remain within validated limits. The warning state is generated when one or more
features exceed a warning threshold for a defined persistence period. The critical state is generated
when multiple features exceed their critical thresholds or when a severe individual condition is
detected.

A simple sensor-fusion score can be used to combine the two measurement domains:
S = w_c C + w_v V, where w_c + w_v = 1
Here, C and V are normalized current and vibration condition indicators. The weights can be
selected during validation rather than assumed in advance. A rule-based alternative can also be used
where the current and vibration channels are treated separately and the final state is determined by
logical conditions. The latter approach is attractive for a first industrial implementation because it is
transparent to maintenance personnel.
3.7 HMI and Data Logging
The HMI screen is designed to provide the information required by an operator or maintenance
engineer without exposing unnecessary signal-processing detail. The main screen displays motor
running status, current RMS, vibration RMS, condition state, alarm state and last maintenance
indication. Trend screens show changes over time. Alarm history records the time, motor
identification, abnormal variable and condition level. This arrangement supports maintenance
decisions while retaining the simplicity of a PLC-based control system.
3.8 Experimental Validation Procedure
Validation should be performed using a motor test rig or an operational industrial motor where
controlled fault simulation is safe and authorized. A healthy baseline is first collected under several
load levels. The motor is then evaluated under selected abnormal conditions such as controlled
imbalance, misalignment, increased mechanical load or simulated sensor abnormalities. Electrical
faults should only be introduced using a safe laboratory arrangement and under appropriate
supervision.
• Stage 1: Record healthy motor data at no-load and representative load points.
• Stage 2: Record current and vibration signals simultaneously.
• Stage 3: Calculate baseline feature ranges and statistical limits.
• Stage 4: Introduce controlled abnormal conditions one at a time.
• Stage 5: Compare current-only, vibration-only and fused decisions.
• Stage 6: Measure detection time, false-alarm rate and classification consistency.
• Stage 7: Implement validated limits in PLC logic and repeat the test in real time.

4.1 Condition Indicator Behaviour
Under healthy operation, both current and vibration indicators should remain within a relatively stable
baseline range for a constant operating condition. When mechanical degradation is introduced, the
vibration channel is expected to provide an earlier indication of some mechanical faults, while current
features may provide complementary evidence when the fault changes the motor's electromagnetic
loading. Electrical abnormalities are expected to produce a stronger response in the current channel.

The following metrics provide a direct comparison between monitoring strategies:

• Detection rate: proportion of known abnormal conditions correctly identified.
• False-alarm rate: proportion of healthy operating windows incorrectly classified as abnormal.
• Detection latency: time between the onset of a validated abnormal condition and the PLC alarm.
• Diagnostic agreement: consistency between the fused decision and the known test condition.
• Availability of monitoring: percentage of operating time during which valid sensor data are

4.4 Proposed Alarm Response
The most useful outcome of the system is not simply a numerical sensor reading but a maintenance-
oriented decision. For example, a gradual increase in vibration accompanied by a stable current level
may initiate a mechanical inspection rather than an immediate motor replacement. Conversely, a
persistent current imbalance with comparatively normal vibration may lead to electrical checks
involving supply balance, connections, protection devices or windings. When both channels deteriorate
simultaneously, the system can raise a higher-level maintenance priority. Final trip settings should
remain separate from diagnostic warning settings and must be validated against the machine
manufacturer's protection requirements.

5. DISCUSSION
The proposed architecture combines two signals that describe different aspects of motor behaviour.
Current measurements provide an electrical view and are closely linked to electromagnetic loading,
while vibration measurements provide a mechanical view. Their combination is therefore more
informative than treating either channel as a complete representation of motor health. This agrees with
published research showing that current and vibration features can be combined for induction-motor
diagnosis (Gangsar and Tiwari, 2020; Yang and Kim, 2006).
A major advantage of the PLC-based approach is practical integration. In a manufacturing plant, PLCs
already control motors and interact with sensors, drives, contactors, protection devices and operator
interfaces. Implementing the condition logic at the control level can reduce the separation between
maintenance monitoring and process automation. The proposed system can also be expanded to
multiple motors by assigning each motor an identification, baseline profile and set of diagnostic limits.
Another important consideration is the influence of operating conditions. A motor operating at high
load naturally draws more current and may produce different vibration levels than the same motor
under light load. Similarly, a VFD changes the electrical spectrum. Consequently, fixed thresholds
should not be selected only from a single healthy measurement. Baselines should be collected over the
normal operating envelope. Where the process permits, the PLC can use operating state, speed or load
information to select the appropriate threshold group.
The architecture also provides a path toward more advanced methods. Once a reliable data-logging
structure has been established, the stored feature vectors can be used for statistical trend analysis or
machine-learning models. However, machine learning should not be introduced simply for complexity.
A transparent rule-based model may be more appropriate during initial deployment because
technicians can understand why an alarm was generated and can adjust thresholds after engineering
validation.

5.1 Practical Industrial Benefits
• Earlier identification of abnormal motor behaviour.
• Reduced dependence on periodic manual inspection for continuously operating equipment.
• Improved maintenance planning through trend information.
• Use of existing PLC/HMI infrastructure for condition information.
• Potential reduction in unplanned downtime when alarms are acted upon appropriately.
• Creation of a structured historical database for future predictive-maintenance development.
5.2 Limitations
The proposed study has several limitations. First, the diagnostic thresholds are motor- and application-
dependent and cannot be transferred directly between machines without validation. Second, vibration
measurements are sensitive to sensor location, mounting method and environmental noise. Third,
current signatures can be affected by VFD switching, power-quality disturbances and rapid load
changes. Fourth, the PLC may not be suitable for processing very high-frequency raw vibration signals
directly. In such cases, a local signal-processing module should calculate validated features before the
PLC receives them.
Another limitation is that the architecture does not by itself guarantee fault identification. Condition
monitoring provides evidence that an operating condition has changed; maintenance personnel still
need to confirm the physical cause before repair. Future research should therefore investigate longer-
term datasets, automated trend estimation and fault-severity classification.
5.3 Future Directions
Future work can extend the system using additional measurements such as motor temperature, voltage
imbalance, rotational speed and power factor. A PLC-connected edge-computing unit could perform
higher-resolution FFT and time-frequency analysis while the PLC remains responsible for real-time
interlocking and alarm management. A later version could compare rule-based fusion with supervised
machine learning using labelled current and vibration features. The resulting system could support a
plant-wide predictive-maintenance database for critical motor-driven assets.
6. CONCLUSION
This paper presented a PLC-based multi-sensor condition-monitoring framework for industrial motors
using electrical current and vibration signals. The proposed architecture addresses a practical weakness
of single-signal monitoring by combining electrical and mechanical evidence before generating a
maintenance decision. Current RMS and spectral features provide information about electrical loading
and electromechanical behaviour, while vibration features provide complementary information about
mechanical condition.
The proposed methodology integrates sensors, signal conditioning, feature extraction, PLC decision
logic and HMI visualization into a single industrial-oriented framework. Rather than depending on a
high-cost specialist monitoring platform, the approach is designed to make use of automation
infrastructure already present in many plants. A structured experimental procedure has also been
defined so that detection rate, false alarms, detection latency and diagnostic agreement can be
measured objectively.
The study therefore provides a foundation for implementing condition monitoring on critical motor-
driven equipment such as pumps, compressors, fans and conveyors. The next stage of the work should
be experimental validation using a controlled motor test rig or approved industrial equipment, followed
by calibration of thresholds and long-term trend monitoring. Once a reliable dataset is available,
