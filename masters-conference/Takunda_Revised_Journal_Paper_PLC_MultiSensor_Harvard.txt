Abstract
Industrial induction motors are essential prime movers in manufacturing plants, and failure of a single motor can
interrupt a complete process section. Conventional preventive maintenance is commonly based on calendar
intervals, operating hours and technician experience. Although useful, this approach does not continuously
reflect the actual condition of the machine. This paper develops a practical PLC-based multi-sensor condition-
monitoring framework that combines motor electrical current and mechanical vibration measurements. The
proposed architecture separates sensing, signal conditioning, feature extraction, PLC decision-making and
HMI/SCADA visualization. Current RMS, phase-current imbalance, vibration RMS, peak value, crest factor and
selected frequency-domain components are used as condition indicators. A transparent sensor-fusion method is
introduced in which normalized electrical and vibration indicators are combined before a normal, warning or
critical state is assigned. The paper also provides worked calculations for RMS current, current imbalance,
vibration RMS, crest factor, FFT frequency resolution and a weighted condition score. These calculations
demonstrate how raw measurements can be converted into engineering quantities suitable for PLC
implementation. An experimental validation procedure is defined for healthy operation, controlled mechanical
faults, increased load and selected electrical abnormalities, with detection rate, false-alarm rate, detection
latency and monitoring availability proposed as evaluation metrics. The approach is intended for industrial
applications where PLC and HMI infrastructure already exists and where a specialist condition-monitoring
platform may be economically or technically impractical. The contribution is therefore not a claim of completed
fault-classification performance, but a reproducible engineering framework that can be implemented and
experimentally validated on industrial motor-driven equipment.
Keywords: PLC; condition monitoring; induction motor; motor current signature analysis; vibration analysis;
multi-sensor fusion; predictive maintenance; industrial automation.
1. Introduction
Electric motors are fundamental to modern production systems because they provide the mechanical power
required by pumps, compressors, fans, conveyors and process machinery. The reliability of these machines is
consequently linked to production availability, maintenance cost and process continuity. Motor condition
monitoring has developed from periodic inspection toward online measurement of electrical, mechanical and
thermal variables. Current signature analysis is particularly attractive because stator current can be measured
without direct access to the rotating shaft, whereas vibration provides a direct indication of mechanical
behaviour (Benbouzid, 2000; Gangsar and Tiwari, 2020).
The limitation of relying on a single measurement is that different fault mechanisms can produce overlapping
symptoms. A rise in vibration may be associated with imbalance, misalignment, looseness or bearing
deterioration, while an increase or distortion in current may result from loading, supply imbalance, electrical
faults or electromechanical coupling. Research has therefore investigated combinations of current and vibration
information for online diagnosis (Yang and Kim, 2006). More recent reviews also show continued development
of current-signature methods, including approaches for variable-speed operation where fixed spectral features
can become difficult to interpret (Niu, 2023; Yakhni et al., 2023).
Industrial automation provides an opportunity to move part of this diagnostic process into equipment that
already exists in many factories. PLCs are routinely used for motor starting, sequencing, interlocking, protection
signals and process supervision. Integrating condition indicators with the PLC therefore reduces the separation
between production control and maintenance information. The proposed work addresses this practical
implementation problem by developing a PLC-centred architecture in which current and vibration information
are processed into transparent condition indicators.

Many industrial maintenance programmes still depend on fixed inspection intervals or respond after abnormal

1.2 Aim and Objectives
The aim is to develop and define a PLC-based multi-sensor condition-monitoring system for industrial induction
motors using electrical current and vibration signals.
• Develop a sensing architecture for simultaneous acquisition of motor current and vibration.
• Define time-domain and frequency-domain features suitable for condition assessment.
• Develop a transparent PLC decision and sensor-fusion method for normal, warning and critical states.
• Develop an HMI/SCADA presentation structure for trends, alarms and maintenance information.
• Define a controlled experimental procedure for comparing current-only, vibration-only and fused
monitoring.
• Establish objective performance measures including detection rate, false-alarm rate and detection latency.
2. Literature Review and Research Gap
Condition monitoring methods for induction motors include temperature measurement, insulation testing,
current signature analysis, vibration analysis, magnetic-flux monitoring, acoustic techniques and combinations
of these signals. Gangsar and Tiwari (2020) reviewed signal-based monitoring and emphasized the value of both
vibration and current measurements for electrical and mechanical fault diagnosis. Benbouzid (2000) established
the basis of motor current signature analysis as a means of identifying abnormal conditions from the spectral
content of stator current. These studies support the use of current as an online, non-invasive measurement
channel.
Vibration monitoring provides complementary mechanical information. Bearing defects, imbalance,
misalignment and looseness can change both the magnitude and frequency content of the vibration signal. Yang
and Kim (2006) demonstrated a data-fusion approach using vibration and current features for induction-motor
fault diagnosis, providing a direct precedent for combining the two measurement domains. The advantage of the
present work is to translate this multi-sensor principle into an automation-oriented PLC architecture rather than
treating the diagnostic system only as an offline signal-processing exercise.
Variable-speed operation introduces an additional challenge. Yakhni et al. (2023) note that transient motor-
current signatures become more difficult to interpret when the operating point changes. VFD switching, load
variation and speed changes can alter the measured spectrum. Therefore, the proposed system does not depend
on one universal frequency peak. Instead, the operating state, baseline condition and multiple features are
considered together.

2.2 Research Gap
The literature establishes that current and vibration contain complementary information, but there remains a
practical implementation gap between advanced diagnostic research and the control architecture available on
industrial floors. Many systems rely on specialist acquisition hardware, offline analysis or computationally
intensive algorithms. The proposed research focuses on the intermediate engineering layer: extracting a small
but meaningful set of indicators and implementing transparent decision logic at PLC level. This creates a
pathway from conventional preventive maintenance toward condition-based maintenance without requiring
immediate deployment of a complex machine-learning platform.
3. Proposed System Architecture
The system is organised into five functional layers: sensing, signal conditioning, feature extraction, PLC
decision-making and operator visualization. The architecture is designed so that the PLC receives reliable
condition indicators rather than attempting to process every high-frequency raw vibration sample. This is
important because a conventional PLC is primarily a real-time control device, whereas high-resolution vibration
processing can require significantly higher sampling and computational resources.
Figure 1. Proposed PLC-based multi-sensor condition-monitoring architecture. The figure represents the proposed design and is not
a measured experimental result.
Current is measured using a suitable current transformer or Hall-effect sensor, depending on the motor rating
and electrical arrangement. Vibration is measured using an accelerometer mounted consistently on the motor
housing, preferably near the bearing location and in a defined radial direction. Sensor signals are isolated,
filtered and scaled before analogue-to-digital conversion. Where the required vibration bandwidth is beyond the
PLC analogue-input capability, an edge acquisition module can calculate features such as RMS, peak and
spectral indicators before sending them to the PLC.

4. Signal Processing and Engineering Calculations
The monitoring system converts sampled signals into numerical features that can be interpreted by the PLC. The
following calculations are central to the proposed method. Worked examples are included using explicitly
illustrative data; they are not claimed to be measurements from the author's motor test rig.
Figure 2. Proposed signal-processing chain from raw measurements to maintenance-oriented PLC decisions.
4.1 Current RMS
For a sampled current waveform containing N samples, the RMS current is calculated as:
I_RMS = √[(1/N) Σ(i_k²)], k = 1, 2, …, N
Consider the illustrative five-sample sequence 6.2, 6.5, 6.1, 6.4 and 6.3 A. The mean current is 6.30 A and the
calculated RMS value is approximately 6.302 A. The RMS value provides a compact representation of current
loading, but it should not be interpreted as a fault indicator by itself because a healthy motor can legitimately
draw different current at different loads.
4.2 Three-Phase Current Imbalance
For three-phase measurements, a simple percentage current-imbalance indicator can be defined as the maximum
deviation from the average divided by the average:
I_imbalance (%) = [max|I_phase − I_avg| / I_avg] × 100

As an illustrative example, if the three measured phase currents are 6.2, 6.4 and 6.0 A, the average is 6.20 A and
the maximum deviation is 0.20 A. The resulting imbalance is approximately 3.23%. This calculation can be
implemented in the PLC using three analogue channels and a small arithmetic routine.
4.3 Vibration RMS and Crest Factor
Vibration RMS represents the overall energy of the measured acceleration signal. For N samples:
V_RMS = √[(1/N) Σ(x_k²)]
The crest factor provides an additional indicator of impulsive behaviour:
Crest Factor = Peak |x_k| / V_RMS
Using the illustrative vibration samples [0.16, −0.19, 0.21, −0.18, 0.17, −0.20, 0.15, −0.22] g gives V_RMS ≈
0.186 g and a crest factor of approximately 1.18. An increase in crest factor over a motor-specific baseline can
be useful when developing bearing-fault indicators because impulsive events may increase peak acceleration
more rapidly than overall RMS.
4.4 Kurtosis
Kurtosis can be used to identify departures from an approximately Gaussian vibration distribution. A simplified
population form is:
K = [ (1/N) Σ(x_k − μ)^4 ] / σ^4
Because kurtosis is sensitive to outliers, it should be interpreted with adequate sample length and consistent
sensor mounting. It is proposed here as a supplementary feature rather than a stand-alone alarm variable.
4.5 Fast Fourier Transform and Frequency Resolution
The FFT converts a sampled signal from the time domain into frequency-domain components. For a sampling
frequency F_s and transform length N, the nominal frequency-bin spacing is:
Δf = F_s / N
For an illustrative acquisition system operating at 10 kHz with N = 2048 samples, Δf = 4.883 Hz. This
calculation is important when selecting the acquisition parameters because insufficient frequency resolution can
make neighbouring spectral components difficult to separate. The sampling frequency must also be sufficiently
high relative to the highest frequency of interest to avoid aliasing.
4.6 Normalised Condition Indicators
To combine current and vibration measurements, each feature can be normalised against a healthy baseline. A
simple engineering form is:
C = clip[(X_c − X_base) / (X_warn − X_base), 0, 1]
V = clip[(X_v − V_base) / (V_warn − V_base), 0, 1]
Here C and V are normalized electrical and vibration indicators. The clip function restricts the value to the
interval 0–1 for the basic implementation. Motor-specific baselines are preferred because motor size, mounting,
speed and load affect the measured values.
4.7 Sensor-Fusion Score
A transparent weighted fusion score can then be calculated as:
S = w_c C + w_v V, where w_c + w_v = 1
As a worked example only, if C = 0.65, V = 0.85, w_c = 0.40 and w_v = 0.60, then S = 0.77. If an engineering
validation programme defined S < 0.60 as normal, 0.60–0.80 as warning and S > 0.80 as critical, the worked

5. PLC Decision Logic and HMI Design
The PLC is responsible for deterministic classification, persistence timing, alarm handling and communication
with the HMI. The design deliberately separates diagnostic warning thresholds from protective trip functions. A
condition-monitoring alarm should not automatically become a motor trip unless the threshold has been
validated for that specific machine and integrated with the approved protection philosophy.

5.1 Persistence and False-Alarm Control
Industrial measurements can contain short disturbances caused by switching, load changes or mechanical
shocks. A practical PLC algorithm should therefore use persistence rather than reacting to one sample. For
example, a warning may require the abnormal condition to remain present for several consecutive feature
windows. The persistence time should be selected from the machine dynamics and the required response time
rather than chosen arbitrarily.
5.2 HMI Information Structure
• Motor identification and running status.
• Current RMS and, where available, three-phase current imbalance.

6. Experimental Validation Method
The current manuscript is intentionally structured as a reproducible experimental design rather than presenting
unmeasured values as results. A laboratory motor test rig or an approved industrial motor can be used. The
motor should be operated first in a healthy state over a defined operating envelope, after which controlled
abnormal conditions can be introduced one at a time. Electrical fault creation should only be performed using a
safe laboratory arrangement under competent supervision.
6.1 Experimental Stages
• Record healthy current and vibration data at no-load and representative load points.
• Record motor speed or VFD frequency simultaneously with the sensor data.
• Calculate baseline mean, standard deviation and selected percentiles for each feature.
• Introduce one controlled abnormal condition at a time, such as imbalance, misalignment or increased load.
• Repeat measurements at more than one operating point to test robustness.
• Compare current-only, vibration-only and fused decisions.
• Measure detection rate, false-alarm rate, detection latency and monitoring availability.
• Transfer validated thresholds and logic to the PLC and repeat the test in real time.

7.1 Detection Metrics
Once the experiment is completed, each monitoring window can be classified as normal or abnormal and
compared with the known test condition. Detection rate, precision, false-alarm rate and latency should be
calculated from the resulting confusion matrix.
Detection rate = TP / (TP + FN)
False-alarm rate = FP / (FP + TN)
Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
Detection latency = t_alarm − t_fault_onset

These measures should be calculated separately for each fault class and operating condition rather than reporting
one combined number only. A system that performs well at constant speed but generates excessive alarms
during VFD operation should not be treated as equally reliable across the entire operating envelope.
7.2 Worked Example of Classification Metrics
To demonstrate the calculation method only, assume a hypothetical validation set contains 20 abnormal
windows and 30 healthy windows. If 18 abnormal windows are correctly detected, 2 abnormal windows are
missed, and 3 healthy windows generate false alarms, then TP = 18, FN = 2, FP = 3 and TN = 27. The
illustrative detection rate is therefore 90.0%, while the false-alarm rate is 10.0% and precision is 85.7%. These
numbers are mathematical examples and must not be reported as experimental performance.
7.3 Monitoring Availability
Monitoring availability (%) = [valid monitoring time / scheduled monitoring time] × 100
This metric is important for an industrial deployment because a theoretically accurate algorithm is of limited
practical value if sensor faults, communication interruptions or data-quality problems frequently remove it from
service.
8. Discussion
The proposed architecture combines electrical and mechanical evidence rather than assuming that one
measurement can explain every motor fault. Current information is valuable because it can be measured non-
invasively and is related to electromagnetic loading, while vibration responds more directly to mechanical
phenomena. The complementary nature of the signals is consistent with published research on combined current
and vibration diagnosis (Yang and Kim, 2006; Gangsar and Tiwari, 2020).
The PLC-centred implementation is particularly relevant to manufacturing environments because PLCs are
already connected to motors, drives, protection devices and operator interfaces. The proposed architecture can
therefore be added as a condition-monitoring layer without replacing the existing process-control system. Where
raw vibration processing exceeds PLC capability, the architecture allows feature extraction to be performed at
the edge while leaving deterministic alarm logic in the PLC.
An important engineering issue is threshold selection. A single universal vibration or current limit can produce
poor results because machines operate under different loads, speeds, mounting conditions and process duties.
ISO 20816-1 emphasizes that vibration evaluation depends on defined measurement conditions and operating
state (ISO, 2016). Similarly, current signatures can change with variable speed and transient operation, which
supports the use of speed-aware or operating-state-aware baselines (Yakhni et al., 2023).
The approach also offers a staged pathway toward predictive analytics. A first implementation can use
transparent rules and trends. Once sufficient labelled data are accumulated, statistical models or machine-
learning methods can be evaluated against the rule-based baseline. This avoids introducing artificial intelligence
before the measurement system, data quality and maintenance workflow are reliable.
8.1 Practical Industrial Benefits
• Earlier visibility of abnormal motor behaviour.
• Reduced dependence on intermittent manual inspection for continuously operating assets.
• Better planning of maintenance interventions using trend information.
• Use of existing PLC/HMI infrastructure rather than a completely separate monitoring platform.
• Structured historical data for future predictive-maintenance development.
• Potential reduction in unplanned downtime when alarms are acted upon appropriately.

8.2 Limitations
The principal limitation of the present manuscript is that the experimental campaign has not yet been completed;
therefore, no numerical detection accuracy is claimed. Other limitations include sensor mounting sensitivity,
environmental vibration, load dependence, VFD switching effects and the computational limits of conventional
PLCs. Thresholds must be validated separately for each motor or motor class. Condition monitoring also
provides evidence of abnormal behaviour rather than automatically proving the physical root cause.
Maintenance personnel must therefore confirm the fault before major repair or replacement.
9. Implementation Considerations for Industrial Deployment
For a manufacturing plant, the first deployment should focus on critical motor-driven assets where downtime
has a clear operational consequence. A practical pilot can begin with one motor, one current-sensing channel
and one accelerometer, followed by expansion to multiple assets after the baseline and alarm logic have been
validated.

10. Conclusion
This paper developed a PLC-based multi-sensor condition-monitoring framework for industrial induction
motors using electrical current and vibration signals. The principal engineering proposition is that
complementary electrical and mechanical evidence can be converted into transparent condition indicators and
evaluated through a PLC-based decision layer.
The revised methodology strengthens the original concept by defining the complete measurement chain, sensor-
placement discipline, feature extraction equations, normalization procedure, weighted sensor-fusion score, PLC
state logic, HMI requirements and experimental validation procedure. Worked calculations demonstrate how
current RMS, phase-current imbalance, vibration RMS, crest factor and FFT resolution can be obtained from
sampled signals.
The paper deliberately avoids presenting illustrative plots or hypothetical classification metrics as measured
results. The next research stage is therefore experimental implementation on a controlled motor test rig or
approved industrial equipment. After collection of healthy and controlled-fault datasets, the proposed thresholds,
fusion weights and PLC logic can be calibrated and the system can be evaluated using detection rate, false-alarm
rate, precision, detection latency and monitoring availability.
